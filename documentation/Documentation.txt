Acoustic Threat Detection for Environments Cameras Cannot Cover

Project Documentation

Author:        Zarwasham Lodhi
Co-authors:    Hamza Khan, Ibad Ur Rehman, Sarim Owais

===============================================================================
CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
===============================================================================

1.1 Project Overview

SonicSentinel AI listens. It takes audio from a live microphone or from an
uploaded recording, decides what it is hearing across ten safety relevant sound
classes, and raises an alert when something needs a human to look at it.

The system does this twice, independently. One classifier runs on the server in
Python. A second, completely separate classifier runs inside the browser tab.
Neither is ever shown the other's answer. Where the two agree, the result is
treated as confident. Where they disagree, the event is flagged for a person
rather than quietly guessed at.

See: reports/plots/architecture_diagram.png

1.2 Problem Statement

Camera systems are everywhere and they are blind to most of what matters. A
camera pointed at a corridor does not know that a window broke in the room
behind it. It cannot hear a scream from a stairwell outside its cone of view, or
a machine beginning to fail in a plant room at three in the morning. Sound
carries around corners and through walls. Video does not.

1.3 Background

Audio classification has become practical on ordinary hardware. Pretrained audio
networks such as YAMNet expose general purpose embeddings that can be adapted to
a specific set of classes with a modest amount of labelled data. At the same
time, TensorFlow.js has made it possible to run a small convolutional network
inside a browser tab at real time speed. Those two facts together are what make
a two model design realistic for a project of this size.

1.4 Business Necessity

A single model that is confidently wrong is worse than no model. In a safety
setting, a false alarm costs attention and a missed event costs more than that.
Running two independent classifiers and comparing them turns a silent failure
into a visible disagreement, which is something an operator can act on.

1.5 Proposed Solution

A Flask web application with:

  - audio upload and live microphone capture
  - a Python classifier on the server
  - a second classifier running in the browser on the same audio
  - an agreement and severity layer that decides what becomes an alert
  - a manual review queue for anything uncertain
  - event history, reporting and role based access

1.6 Objectives

  - classify ten sound classes from one second of audio
  - keep the two classifiers genuinely independent
  - meet the accuracy and recall targets set out in the SRS
  - keep all audio on the machine that recorded it

CHAPTER 2. SYSTEM ANALYSIS
===============================================================================

2.1 Existing System

Most deployed monitoring is video first. Where audio exists it is usually
recorded for later review rather than classified as it arrives.

2.2 Problems in Existing System

  - line of sight limits what a camera can observe
  - recorded audio is only useful after an incident, not during one
  - a single model gives no signal about its own reliability

2.3 Proposed System

Two classifiers on the same audio, compared after the fact. Detections, scores,
agreement status and review decisions are all written to a local database.

2.4 Scope

In scope: classification, alerting, review, reporting, local storage.
Out of scope: speaker identification, transcription, multi site deployment.

2.5 Assumptions

  - audio arrives at 16 kHz mono after preprocessing
  - the browser supports Web Audio and WebGL
  - a single machine hosts both the server and the database

2.6 Constraints

  - the browser model must be small enough to load and run in a tab
  - no audio may leave the local machine
  - the browser model must be reproducible from the same features the
    server pipeline produces

CHAPTER 3. REQUIREMENTS
===============================================================================

3.1 Functional Requirements

  FR1   upload audio files and analyse them
  FR2   capture live microphone audio in fixed windows
  FR3   classify every segment across all ten classes
  FR4   run a second independent classifier in the browser
  FR5   compare the two results and record an agreement status
  FR6   apply severity rules and raise alerts
  FR7   queue uncertain events for manual review
  FR8   store detections, scores and audit records
  FR9   role based access for admin, operator, reviewer and maintenance
  FR10  export detections and show reporting

3.2 Non-Functional Requirements

NFR 4 sets the accuracy bar and is the one the models are measured against:

  - overall test accuracy at or above 85 percent
  - macro F1 at or above 0.80
  - recall at or above 85 percent for each of Gunshot, Glass Breaking,
    Panic Scream, Aggression and Person Asking for Help

3.3 Hardware Requirements

A standard laptop. No GPU is needed for inference. Training was done on Google
Colab.

3.4 Software Requirements

Python 3.11, Flask, librosa, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, TensorFlow.js, SQLite.


===============================================================================
CHAPTER 4. SYSTEM DESIGN
===============================================================================

4.1 System Architecture

See: reports/plots/architecture_diagram.png

Audio enters from one of two places, the microphone or an upload. It is
preprocessed once into 16 kHz mono and cut into three second segments. That same
audio then goes down two paths that never touch each other. The server extracts
features and runs the Python model. The browser computes its own log-mel
spectrogram and runs the browser model.

The browser posts its scores to /api/gtm/<detection_id> only after the server has
already stored its own decision. This ordering is deliberate. It is what
guarantees the browser result cannot have been influenced by the server one.

4.2 Module Description

  audio_preprocessing/   loading, resampling, segmentation, validation
  feature_extraction/    features, spectrograms, embeddings, log-mel
  src/                   training, analysis, comparison, decision, rules
  static/js/             browser model loading, features, inference
  templates/             the web interface
  database/              SQLite schema and access

4.3 Data Flow

  audio -> preprocessing -> segments
        -> server features  -> Python model  -> scores
        -> browser log-mel  -> browser model -> scores
        -> comparison -> severity -> alert, review or log

4.4 Decision Flow

See: reports/plots/decision_flow_diagram.png

The agreement status takes one of four values: Agreement, Acceptable Match,
Model Disagreement, or Awaiting GTM while the browser result has not arrived.


===============================================================================
CHAPTER 5. DATASET AND AUDIO PROCESSING
===============================================================================

5.1 Dataset Classes

Ten classes, in the order used by the label encoder:

  aggression, alarm_siren, animal_sound, background_noise, glass_breaking,
  gunshot, machinery_fault, panic_scream, person_asking_help, vehicle_horn

Five of these are critical: gunshot, glass_breaking, panic_scream, aggression
and person_asking_help.

5.2 Dataset Collection

Clips were gathered per class and stored under audio_dataset/. Duplicate file
contents were checked by hash so that the same audio could not appear under two
different labels.

5.3 Dataset Split

  training      2096 files
  validation     459 files
  testing        445 files
  total         3000 files

The split is group aware by recording. A hash check across all three folders
confirmed 3000 unique files with zero duplicates and zero overlap between
splits. The testing folder was never used for training, tuning, early stopping
or model selection.

See: reports/plots/dataset_distribution.png

5.4 Data Augmentation

An augmented training set sits alongside the original training folder and is
used only for training the browser model. Validation and testing are never
augmented.

5.5 Audio Preprocessing

Mono conversion, resampling to 16 kHz, normalisation, silence trimming, optional
noise reduction, then segmentation into three second windows with padding or
truncation to a fixed length.

5.6 Feature Extraction

Three feature families are used by different models:

  - hand crafted features: MFCC statistics, mel-dB statistics, chroma, zero
    crossing rate, RMS and spectral shape descriptors
  - YAMNet embeddings: 1024 mean pooled and 1024 max pooled, 2048 in total
  - log-mel spectrograms: 31 frames by 64 mel bins, used by the browser model

See: reports/plots/feature_pipeline_diagram.png
See: reports/plots/example_spectrograms.png

CHAPTER 6. MACHINE LEARNING, SERVER SIDE
===============================================================================

6.1 Pipeline

Features are extracted once and cached. Every candidate is fitted on the
training split only. Validation selects the winner. The test split is scored
once, after the choice has been made.

6.2 Models Tested

Five server side models were trained and compared, which satisfies the SRS
requirement for at least three.

YAMNet transfer. A scikit-learn head on frozen YAMNet embeddings, selected on

their own matrices, with a largest deviation of 4.8e-05.

CHAPTER 7. THE BROWSER MODEL
===============================================================================

7.1 First Attempt: Google Teachable Machine

The original second classifier was built with Google Teachable Machine. It
exports directly to TensorFlow.js, which made it an obvious starting point for
a model that has to run in a browser tab.

7.2 The Platform Limitation

Teachable Machine caps how much audio a class can be trained on. In practice the
project could supply only around 100 one second samples per class before the
tool would take no more. With ten classes that is roughly 1000 training samples
in total, against a dataset of 3000 recordings that had already been collected
and cleaned.

The consequence is that most of the dataset simply could not be used. A class
with 255 usable training recordings had to be reduced to 100 single second
excerpts, one per recording, chosen by loudness. Everything else was discarded
before training even began. The platform also gives no control over the training
schedule, no class weighting, no early stopping criterion and no way to inspect
what the network learned.

7.3 Teachable Machine Results

Three separate Teachable Machine models were exported and two were measured
against the full test split:

  Export 1    35.14% accuracy    macro F1 0.3460
  Export 2    36.04% accuracy    macro F1 0.3425

Against a ten class problem where chance is 10 percent, the model had learned
something, but nowhere near enough. Background noise recall fell to under 9
percent and the model funnelled a large share of every class into gunshot.

This is a limitation of the platform rather than of the approach. Teachable
Machine is built for quick demonstrations with a handful of examples, and it
does that well. It is not built to consume three thousand labelled recordings,
and when the data is there and the tool will not accept it, the ceiling is the
tool.

7.4 Second Attempt: An In-House Browser CNN

The replacement is a small convolutional network trained on the full dataset and
exported to TensorFlow.js. It fills exactly the same role, produces the same
three artefacts in the same folder, and nothing downstream needed to change.

Architecture: four convolution blocks of 32, 64, 128 and 128 filters, each with
batch normalisation, ReLU and max pooling, followed by global average pooling,
dropout at 0.3 and a dense softmax over ten classes. 242,602 parameters, which
loads as a 970 KB weight shard.

Input: a log-mel spectrogram of one second of audio, 31 frames by 64 mel bins.

7.5 Feature Parity

The one thing that can silently destroy a browser model is a mismatch between
the features it was trained on and the features the browser produces at
inference. Two earlier model generations on this project were lost to exactly
that.

So the features were written twice, once in Python and once in JavaScript, and
the two were compared before any training took place:

  backend   mean absolute difference   correlation
  CPU                       3.7e-05      1.000000
  WebGL                      1.7e-03      0.999976

The gate was a mean absolute difference below 0.01 and a correlation above
0.999. Both passed with a wide margin, on both the shared sample comparison and
the full path where the browser decodes the audio file itself.

See: reports/plots/feature_pipeline_diagram.png

7.6 Aggregation Policy

A clip is longer than the one second the model sees, so it is cut into
overlapping windows and the per class scores are combined. Three policies were
compared on the validation split only:

  max over windows          80.39% accuracy   0.7978 macro F1
  mean over windows         82.14% accuracy   0.8153 macro F1
  mean of the top 3 windows 81.48% accuracy   0.8084 macro F1

Mean over windows was selected on validation macro F1 and applied to both the
live and the uploaded path.

scored in Python and again in the browser through the application's own code

against a tolerance of 1e-02.

CHAPTER 8. DETECTION AND ALERT SYSTEM
===============================================================================

8.1 Classification

Each three second segment is scored by both models across all ten classes. The
full score vector is stored, not only the winner.

8.2 Model Agreement

The two score sets are mapped onto canonical class ids and compared. The
agreement status, the confidence difference and the top two margin are all
recorded against the detection.

8.3 Severity and Critical Events

Gunshot, Panic Scream and Person Asking for Help are treated as critical when
the configured confidence and confirmation conditions are met.

8.4 Manual Review

Disagreement between the two models routes the event to a review queue. A
reviewer confirms or corrects it and the decision is written back into the
event history.

See: reports/plots/decision_flow_diagram.png

CHAPTER 14. LIMITATIONS
===============================================================================

14.1 The Browser Model Falls Short of NFR 4

At 75.73 percent accuracy and 0.7555 macro F1 the browser model misses the bar.
The single biggest cause is background_noise, at 0.4222 recall. Twenty six of
its forty five clips are scattered into vehicle_horn, machinery_fault, gunshot
and alarm_siren. Because those false positives inflate other classes, that one
class costs roughly 0.03 of macro F1 on its own. machinery_fault absorbs 70
predictions for 45 true clips.

14.2 Teachable Machine Could Not Use the Dataset

Covered in 7.2. The tool would not accept more than roughly 100 samples per
class, so most of a 3000 clip dataset went unused and the resulting model
plateaued near 36 percent.

14.3 Acoustically Similar Classes

Gunshot and glass_breaking confuse in both directions. Both are short, loud,
broadband transients and one second of audio does not always separate them.

14.4 Measurement Consistency

The YAMNet figures are per segment and the rest are per clip, as noted in 6.5.

===============================================================================
CHAPTER 17. CONCLUSION
===============================================================================

SonicSentinel AI works end to end. Audio comes in from a microphone or a file,
two independent classifiers score it, the system compares them and decides
whether to alert, queue for review or simply log.

The server side model meets every requirement in NFR 4 at 90.02 percent accuracy
and 0.9000 macro F1. The browser side model does not, at 75.73 percent and
0.7555, though it more than doubles what the Teachable Machine model it replaced
achieved.

The honest position is that half the requirement is met and half is not, and the
remaining gap has a known cause and a known fix.
